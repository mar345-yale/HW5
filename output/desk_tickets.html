Reflection

What the Campus Customs agent team did well, where my plan and the system fell short, and what it would take to handle more of the shop's work. Evidence comes from the Ticket 101, Ticket 102, Ticket 103, and Cash tabs on this page, the run log in output/audit_trail.json, and the summary in output/resolved_tickets.json.

1How I evaluate the agent team on each ticket, and why

A ticket is not "good" just because it ends with status resolved. This system can move money and talk to customers, so I judge each ticket on five things, in order of importance:

  1. Safety and rules. Did every payment wait for a human? Did cash stay non-negative? Did the agents follow the shop rules (use desk.date_today, treat a vendor with an open invoice as blocked, send nothing to customers)? A fast answer that breaks one of these is a failure.
  2. Facts. Can every number the agents used be traced to a tool result and a database column? Invented prices or vendors would make the rest meaningless.
  3. Outcome and honest status. Did the ticket move as far as the tools allow, and does its final status describe reality? "Waiting" when something really is waiting is a correct result, not a failure.
  4. Efficiency. Model calls, tokens, and wasted or repeated work, since the project goal was to avoid burning tokens.
  5. Communication. Is the customer draft accurate, free of promises, and useful to a human?

I use this order because a shop can afford a slow or partial answer, but not a wrong payment, an overdraft, or a made-up promise to a customer.

Was staying safe and keeping cash positive the agents' doing, or the system's?

I agree that we stayed safe and that the human checkpoint is what made it work. The Cash tab shows the result: checking went $3,400 → $2,560 → $160, with exactly two payments, both approved by me, and the balance never went below zero. Looking closer at ticket 103, though, I think most of the credit belongs to the design, not the agents' judgment:

  • Inventory recommended restocking 12 hoodies. At the $22 unit cost in pricing, that is $264, but checking only had $160 after tickets 101 and 102. No agent connected the restock to the cash balance. Accounting checked the margin on the sale but not whether the shop could pay for the restock.
  • The shop still can't run out of money, for three reasons: (1) there is no purchase tool, so a restock can only be recommended; (2) every payment pauses for a person; and (3) the MCP pay tools refuse any payment that would leave a negative balance, even after approval.
  • The agents also never raised a bigger cash problem: the next rent of $2,400 is due 2026-10-02 and only $160 is left (the board shows "−$2,240 left if all paid"). The data is right there in list_bills_due, but no ticket asked about it.
So my assessment is that human intervention plus hard limits in the tools kept us safe. The agents were good at following the rules they were given and weak at noticing risks nobody asked about. That is exactly why a person should stay in the loop for anything involving money.

2My predictions compared with what actually happened

My Problem 6 predictions are still on each ticket tab, unchanged, above the "What actually happened" section. Looking back, I described the agents as a fixed sequence of tool calls ("inventory first … then over to accounting"), when the real plans were decisions: who is blocked by what, what needs approval, and what the honest status is. I also didn't predict final statuses or how cash would change, and those turned out to be the most important parts.

Ticket 101: partly right, but I missed the real problem

  • Right: Inventory and Accounting did the core work, Inventory used find_restock_vendors, and a human approval was needed.
  • Missed: I framed it as "we will need to order a shirt." The real blocker was the overdue $840 invoice, because a vendor won't ship while it has an open invoice. Paying that invoice was the decision. I also missed Customer Service, who wrote the reply to Tauhid, and I didn't predict the ending: needs info, because only the customer can choose between waiting and another size.
  • Tool details I got wrong: check_stock wasn't needed (the backend passes stock in with the ticket details); check_margin doesn't apply because no price was in question; the approval came from pay_invoice, not list_payments, which only reads past payments; and Boss's instructions come from boss.md, not delegation.py.

Ticket 102: my best prediction

  • Right: Facilities alone, list_bills_due, then pay_rent only after human approval. This matched almost step for step.
  • Missed: what happens after the payment, when Boss re-checks the ticket and resolves it. That step exists because we chose to re-check after approvals in Problem 8. It's also worth noting that this ticket only worked because of a fix in Problem 5: the first version of Facilities waited until the due date to request rent.

Ticket 103: right agents, wrong decision

  • Right: Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service all took part; Accounting checked margins; Customer Service reported what is available.
  • Missed: I expected a payment ("list_payment … wait for human approval"). No money moved. The real decision was a price: is $50 (13.79% off, still $28 above cost) acceptable? The database has no discount policy, so only a person can say. I also didn't notice that ticket 103 depended on ticket 101: the restock vendor was only unblocked because invoice 501 had already been paid, so the order I ran the tickets in mattered.
What I would do differently: for each ticket, predict (1) the blocking fact, (2) the decision a human has to make, (3) the final status, and (4) the change in cash, and only then the tools. In my write-up the tool names were a detail I focused on too much. The money and the blockers were what mattered.

3What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Ticket 102 clearly, and honestly most of this three-ticket workload.

  • Ticket 102: the whole job was "read the lease, check cash, request rent." One agent with list_bills_due and pay_rent would do it in about 2 model calls. In our team it took 4: Boss passed the task to Facilities and then read Facilities' answer, and that handoff added cost without adding judgment.
  • Handoffs repeat work and context: every delegation re-sends the ticket details and runs another model loop. In ticket 101, Customer Service asked Inventory a question Inventory had already answered for Boss (find_restock_vendors ran again), and a handoff from Inventory to Accounting was refused because the ticket had hit its handoff limit. A single agent would have kept all of that in one context.
  • The shop is small: 9 tables, 3 tickets, and 16 tools fit easily in one agent's context, so splitting the work didn't solve a size problem.
  • The safety doesn't depend on the team: the approval pause, the hidden approved_by, and the no-negative-cash rule all live in the backend and the MCP tools. They would protect a single agent just as well.

Where the team did earn its keep: separation of duties (only Accounting can request invoice payments and only Facilities can request rent, like real job roles); parallel work on tickets 101 and 103; shorter, focused prompts for each role; and a clearer audit trail and dashboard, where you can see who decided what. With hundreds of tickets, more tools, and real staff behind each role, those benefits would grow. For three tickets, one well-prompted agent would have been cheaper and just as safe.

4Three new problems the team could solve with today's tools

A. "Can we cover October rent?" (a real problem in our data right now)

Checking is $160 and the next rent of $2,400 is due 2026-10-02 (see the Cash tab). A ticket like "check upcoming bills against cash" would go to Facilities and Accounting. list_bills_due reports cash_covers_all: false and −$2,240, get_lease confirms the date, and list_payments confirms rent was already paid this month. Facilities would not request pay_rent: the rent isn't due within 7 days, and the pay tool would refuse anyway because cash would go negative. Boss would mark the ticket blocked and give a person a clear shortfall number. The team can find and explain this problem, but it can't fix it (see problem E below).

B. Crest Mugs are out of stock

check_stock shows Crest Mug at 0 on hand. find_restock_vendors points to Elm City Gifts (specialty: mugs and small goods, 3-day lead time, no open invoices, so not blocked), and check_margin shows $14 list against $3.50 cost. Inventory proposes a restock, and Customer Service drafts a note for anyone asking about mugs. This is the same flow as ticket 101, without the blocked vendor.

C. Yale AI Club says "we'll take mixed sizes"

This is a follow-up to ticket 103. If the club accepts 8 M plus 12 L, check_stock shows L has 14, so no restock is needed. check_margin can price the full 20 at list or at the proposed $50. Customer Service drafts the confirmation and Boss puts the $50 price in front of a person. The order could be fully planned without buying anything, which also avoids the $264 restock the shop can't afford.

5Three new problems the team could not solve, and the tools it would need

D. Actually ordering and receiving stock

Why it fails today: tickets 101 and 103 both ended with a restock that is only a recommendation. There is no purchase tool, no record of orders, and no way to add stock when it arrives. That is why 101 can't finish even after its vendor was unblocked.

Tools needed: create_purchase_order(vendor_id, sku, size, qty), which computes the cost from pricing.unit_cost, checks it against cash and upcoming bills (it would have caught the $264 against $160), and pauses for human approval like the pay tools. It would write to a new purchase_orders table. Also receive_stock(po_id), which adds the quantity to inventory and creates the vendor's invoice for Accounting to pay. The dashboard would show purchase orders in the same red approval bar.

E. Bringing money in

Why it fails today: by design, cash only goes out. Problem A shows the result: the team can see the October rent shortfall but has no way to sell anything, record a deposit, or plan ahead.

Tools needed: create_order(customer, items, unit_price) with an approved-price step (which would also unstick ticket 103's $50 decision), record_payment_received(order_id, amount) to add cash and a sales table that removes items from stock, and forecast_cash(days) that combines open bills, rent, and expected sales, so Accounting can warn about shortfalls before they happen.

F. Closing the loop with customers

Why it fails today: ticket 101 is waiting on Tauhid, but drafts are never sent and the board has no way to record a reply, so the ticket stays needs info forever. New requests also can't enter the system; all three tickets were already in the database.

Tools needed: send_draft(draft_id, approved_by), which sends only after a human approves the exact text, the way payments work today; log_customer_reply(ticket_id, text), which reopens the ticket so Boss re-checks it, like our re-check after payment; and create_ticket(type, requester, details) for new requests from email or the counter. The customer's text should be treated as untrusted data in prompts, as the Safety section of harness.md recommends.

Bottom line: the team was accurate, followed the rules, and was safe with money, largely because people and the tools set hard limits rather than because of the agents' own judgment. Only ticket 102 truly finished. Tickets 101 and 103 stopped exactly where our tools stop: buying stock, selling at an approved price, and talking to customers. Those three tool gaps are the next things to build.
